About this deck

Vibecoding workshop.

A 90-minute session that takes people from prompting to building working, data-driven tools in Gemini Canvas. This page is the deck's working memory: what it is, how it's made, and what changed and why.

What this is

Thirty-three slides for a hands-on workshop, for staff across all departments, with no coding experience assumed. People arrive with a laptop and leave having built something that pulls live data off the internet and hands it to a model to reason over.

The deck is at peter-guillam123.github.io/vibecoding-workshop. It's driven entirely by hand: arrow keys or space to advance, nothing on a timer, nothing that advances itself. One slide is built to sit still rather than perform, which turns out to matter more than any of the animation.

  • Slide 24 is the activity brief, and it stays on screen while people work.
  • Every prompt card copies on click, and so do the suggestion cards. The lesson plan says to keep the prompts open in a notes file so you're not retyping them live; this removes that step, because the deck is the clipboard. Slide 4 explains it, with a card that demonstrates itself.

How it's made

Plain HTML and CSS, no build step and no framework. It's built from the Guardian deck template, which supplies a <deck-stage> web component for navigation and scaling, a design system carrying the real Guardian font cuts, and a choreography engine that handles the reveals. Slides are markup; new components get appended to the stylesheet rather than forked into the page.

It deploys to GitHub Pages on every push. The fonts load from Guardian servers, which means the deck wants a network connection to look right, though it degrades to Georgia rather than breaking.

One thing worth saying plainly, because it's the point of the workshop as much as the deck: this was built by describing it in English to Claude Code and letting it do the typing.

Diary

Change28 July 2026

Two questions before you share anything

The sharing slide had one warning on it, about who can see the link. It now has a checklist of two, and the new one is the more important.

Is it ready to be shared? Building something that works in twenty minutes is a thrill, and it is not the same as having something tested. The whole session is designed to make people feel how fast this is, so it owes them the counterweight: if the thing does anything that matters, spend real time checking it, look hard at what it actually outputs, and show it to one person who knows the subject before going wider.

Who should it be shared with? The old warning, sharpened. Named people rather than anyone with the link, and the test that matters: everyone you send it to must already have access to whatever data is inside it. Sharing the app must never become a way of sharing the data.

Change29 July 2026

Five rules, where there used to be one feature

Slide 6 existed to demonstrate one thing: that every prompt in the deck copies on click. It did it nicely, with a pointer that drifted in and clicked on a loop. But running the workshop surfaced four things that matter more to whether somebody gets stuck, and none of them had anywhere to live.

So it's five rules now, and the copy trick is only the first. Start a new chat for each project, because the model re-reads your entire conversation every time you ask it something and a chat that has wandered through three projects drags all of it into the fourth. Use a separate chat, Canvas off, for questions and research. Know that Canvas is flaky and that a preview window refusing to load is the tool having a moment rather than your prompt being wrong. And know that starting again is a legitimate move: you cannot argue a drifted project back into shape, and trying wastes more time than rebuilding.

The animated demo came out, along with its stylesheet. The Copied badge still appears inline in rule 01, which was the part that needed showing rather than telling.

Change28 July 2026

One borrowed line, on a yellow slide

Andrej Karpathy, January 2023: the hottest new programming language is English. Seven words that say what the whole session is for, so they now sit at slide 2 with nothing else on the page.

It could have gone on the orientation slide that follows. It shouldn't, because that slide's hero line is the same idea in our own words, and put side by side the two would have fought. One after the other they escalate: the quote states the thesis, and the next slide turns it into a promise about what you'll have built by the end.

Yellow because the deck has spent the whole way through using yellow to mark the bit that matters, on the cover, on the API query, on the thirty minutes in the run sheet. This is that gesture at full scale, used exactly once, which is what stops it being decoration.

Change28 July 2026

Written for the person reading it alone

An unplanned outcome of building the deck this way, with every prompt on the slides and copyable, is that it works as a self-guided tutorial. Someone can be sent the link and get through it without anyone standing at the front. But a reader arriving on their own had no idea what Canvas was, why it rather than anything else, or what they were being promised.

New slide 2 does that job. It opens on the promise, that you'll have built something that works without writing a line of code, with the deck's yellow marker drawing under the last part of it. Then three open columns rather than cards, because this early the page should feel airy: what we're using, what it gives you, and what it isn't.

That third column is the one worth having. Canvas is not a developer's tool, Claude Code and its like go a great deal further and expect more of you, and saying so plainly is more persuasive than pretending otherwise. Canvas is narrower on purpose and already sitting inside Workspace, which is exactly the argument for learning it first. The slide closes by telling anyone reading alone to work through in order and click any prompt to copy it.

Change28 July 2026

Keys, done properly

The session tells people not to paste an API key into Canvas, and then never says what they should do instead, which leaves the most useful half of the internet behind a rule with no workaround. This slide closes that.

The move is to ask for a box in the app where you type your own key each time you use it. The app can reach the API; the key was never written into the code, so there's nothing for anyone opening the app to extract, and Gemini never saw it. There's a copyable prompt for exactly that.

It ends on the bit that people get wrong out of helpfulness rather than carelessness: if you share the app, everyone needs their own key. Sending someone yours to save them the bother means the usage bills to you, it can be used without your knowing, and you can't take it back.

Change28 July 2026

The bug hunt stops depending on one bug

The Newcastle trick was built on Canvas not thinking of it. Running the session showed that it increasingly does: apps now often come out with a disambiguation list already built in, which is a good outcome for the app and a bad one for a slide whose whole premise is that it won't.

So the slide no longer rests on a single reliable failure. It's four questions to ask of your own app, with the specific searches as examples inside them rather than as the headings. Does it match what you know, which is why you start with your own home town. Which one did you mean, for names that turn up in several countries. Do the data sources relate, from the session itself, where Deal loaded the Kent town and Wikipedia supplied the New Deal alongside it. And how does it handle the unexpected, which is less about error messages than about pushing it somewhere it wasn't built for and watching.

The instruction above them is the part that generalises: think about what would trip you up, and ask it exactly that. That works whatever the model has learned to handle by the time this runs again.

Change28 July 2026

Where the facts come from

The workshop ran, and it went well. This is the slide it turned out to need.

Part 2 used to jump straight from "your app lives in a bubble" to "here is an API", which skips the obvious question in between: why not just let the model supply the facts? New slide 10 answers it. An app built on what the model already knows might be perfectly fine, but neither you nor it can tell which parts are still current, because it all came out of training data of uncertain vintage.

Two responses, each with a worked example. Send it looking for a source first: ask for an app about entry requirements for visitors to the UK and you'll get one, assembled from whatever it absorbed; ask it instead for the official gov.uk page and you have something checkable. Or hand it something you already have and can vouch for, a spreadsheet or a PDF, so the app works from that rather than from memory.

The slide ends on the part that actually matters, which is that neither approach is a guarantee. Choosing your own source makes the output verifiable. It doesn't make it true, and anything you build still needs spot-checking against the original.

Milestone27 July 2026

The shaping pass: writing it for the room, not the presenter

A long editing pass the same afternoon, and one theme ran through nearly all of it. The first build was written from the facilitator's chair - build it in front of the room, narrate the loading state, say the useful thing out loud - in a deck that gets handed to everyone who attends. Titles and deks are now doing the job of explaining what's happening to the person reading them.

The prompts went the same way. They started as specifications, full of URL templates, placeholder braces and instructions to return a complete file, which quietly argued against the thing the session exists to prove. They're now sentences anyone could have said out loud. The hook is one deliberately vague line, so thirty people get thirty different apps and thirty different sets of assumptions to argue with, and the slide after it turned into a menu of six things to say next.

Part 5 was a ladder you climbed, which made it about ambition. It's now about understanding what you've made: play with your app, feed it awkward things, find what it gets wrong, and only then refine it. Part 3's debugging slide became a bug hunt that ends on a question to the room rather than a stage direction. And the schedule became a run sheet of durations rather than clock times, so it doesn't lie if the session starts late.

Three things came out entirely. The holding slide, the two appendices of prompts, and the slide answering "doesn't Gemini need a key?" - the appendices because everyone keeps the whole deck, so gathering the prompts in one place solves a problem that no longer exists. The deck ends on "Thanks." rather than a claim about what people arrived able to do.

It now sits behind a light password gate, the same one the Board and Trust deck uses: a hash rather than the password, and an unlock remembered per tab. A deterrent rather than security, which is worth saying plainly given how much of the session is about taking that distinction seriously.

Decision27 July 2026

Internal data: looked into it, left it off the table

Somebody always asks whether they can point one of these at our own numbers rather than a public API. We built a slide offering the Google Sheet route, then took it out again. Writing down why, so nobody has to redo the research.

Technically it works. Both endpoints a browser app would use return proper CORS headers, so a Canvas app really can read a sheet, and both are served with caching explicitly switched off. That settles the live-or-frozen question: paste numbers into a prompt and they're baked into the code at that moment, but point the app at the sheet's address and it re-reads on every load, so you can edit a cell, refresh, and watch the app move.

The sharing is where it falls down. There's no way to have the app read the sheet as the logged-in viewer, because the endpoint refuses credentialed requests. The sheet has to be readable by anyone holding the link. So the safeguard you'd want, that it only reaches people who already have access, can't be enforced by Google's permissions at all: the data is protected by the secrecy of two URLs, one of which is a public Canvas share link. For a newsroom that's not a footnote, it's the whole answer. The option is off the table, and the deck doesn't mention it.

Change27 July 2026

Telling people the prompts are clickable

Every prompt card in the deck has copied itself on click since the first build, and nobody would ever have found out. There's now a slide near the front that says so, with a demonstration rather than an instruction: a real prompt card, a pointer that drifts in and clicks it, and the Copied badge coming up, on a loop. The card is live, so anyone who wants to check can click it themselves while the slide is still up.

Making that promise honest meant widening it. The six suggestion cards in Part 1 looked exactly like things you'd want to paste and weren't copyable, which is worse than not offering it at all. They are now, along with everything else, and the script strips the display quote marks so what lands on the clipboard is the sentence rather than the typography.

Change27 July 2026

Choosing an API is a Part 4 problem, not a Part 3 one

Part 3 was doing two jobs at once. It modelled the build, which is what it's for, but it opened by teaching people how to go and find an API, and closed with two slides of recommended ones. All of that is choosing, and choosing is what you do at the moment you're about to build something, not ten minutes earlier while watching someone else work.

So the list and the discovery prompts have moved into the front of Part 4. The running order there is now: your turn, here are twelve APIs that work, here's how to find your own if none of them appeal, here's the brief. Nobody has to hold a menu in their head across a section break.

Part 3 is better for losing them. It's three clean beats with nothing else in the way: build it, add a second source, hand it to the model. The awkward "beat 2½" from the earlier rework is gone, because the second source is simply beat 2 now.

Change27 July 2026

Explaining the API bit to people who have never met one

Two slides in Part 2 were written as if the audience already knew what they were looking at. They don't, and that's the entire point of the session.

The raw JSON slide showed a web address and a blob of text and asserted that this was the good bit. It now says what the question actually is, in English, before showing the answer: is there a place called Tokyo, and if so, where exactly is it? Beside the response there's a plain reading of it, four lines explaining that quote marks mean text you can print, that a number without quote marks is something you can do maths with, that two letters are all you need to fetch a flag, and that the coordinates can be handed to a weather API. That last one quietly plants the chaining idea three slides before we use it.

The safety briefing was titled "Three things that are actually true", which tells you nothing. It now opens by naming what we've been using all along: everything in today's list is free and keyless, meaning no sign-up and no password, safe to use straight away. Out in the wild, some APIs ask for more.

Rule 01 got most of the new room, because it's the one that matters. What a key actually is, why they exist at all (stopping abuse and overload, and tying usage to whoever registered), and why that makes it yours alone. Then a never-and-instead couplet: never give the key to Gemini or let it write the key into the code, because anyone who opens the app can pull it back out; instead ask Gemini to have the app request the key from whoever is using it. Rule 02 gained the most practical line on the slide, which is that you can simply ask Gemini to explain an API's limits and what you can build with it before you start.

The separate slide answering "doesn't Gemini need a key?" came out. It was answering a question nobody had been given the vocabulary to ask yet, and the useful half of it now lives inside rule 01.

Change27 July 2026

A vaguer prompt, on purpose

The hook prompt was a specification: three named inputs, a working-year assumption, pause and reset buttons, a dark-mode dashboard. It produced a good app and a bad lesson, because everyone in the room would have ended up with more or less the same thing.

It is now one sentence. "Create an app that calculates the cost of a meeting." Thirty people get thirty different apps, which means thirty different sets of assumptions to notice and argue with. Slide 5 now says what Canvas will do next, that the preview opens beside the code, and that your version won't match anyone else's, then asks people to actually use the thing and work out what it decided without asking them.

Slide 6 absorbed everything the old prompt used to specify, but as a menu of six moves rather than a spec: make it live, make it look better, make it honest, make it easier, make it sting, make it useful. Each one is the sentence to type, not a description of it. The advice underneath is to ask for one thing at a time, because a stacked request comes back with most of it done and no clue which part got dropped.

Fix27 July 2026

Canvas is two clicks down, not one

The pre-flight slide sent people to the + button to find Canvas. It isn't there. The path is +, then More tools, then Canvas, and a Canvas chip appears next to the prompt box once it's on. Corrected on slide 2, and on the Part 1 opener, which had inherited "click the Canvas icon" straight from the lesson plan.

The chip is the useful part. It's a confirmation signal you can ask thirty people to check at a glance, which is exactly what a two-minute pre-flight needs.

Milestone27 July 2026

First build, and an API matrix that needed rescuing

The lesson plan's facilitator checklist opens with an instruction to click-test every API in the matrix on the morning of the session, on the grounds that free APIs die without warning. So I did that before writing a single slide. Two of the twelve were broken, and one of them was load-bearing.

REST Countries was dead. It returned a deprecation notice instead of data, and the v5 that replaces it answers Authorization key required. That's worse than a broken link, because REST Countries was the API modelled live in Part 3 and the basis of both the base-build and the LLM-handoff prompts in the appendix. Its replacement also breaks the workshop's own first safety rule, which is never to paste a key into Canvas.

The fix turned out better than the thing it replaced. Instead of one substitute, the deck now models two sources: Open-Meteo's geocoding endpoint for the structured half, meaning country, population, timezone, coordinates and the two-letter code that fetches a flag, and Wikipedia's summary endpoint for the human half, a photograph and a paragraph of prose. Both keyless, both browser-friendly.

Modelling them one after the other adds a beat the plan didn't have, which is watching a second source arrive off the back of a single sentence. It also sharpens the line the whole session is built around, because you can now point at each half on screen while saying that the API gives you the facts and the model gives you judgement.

It came with a gift. Type Newcastle and it breaks, every time: Open-Meteo confidently returns Australia while Wikipedia hands back a disambiguation page with no photograph. That's a reliable, unfrightening bug to demonstrate the panic-button prompt against, rather than waiting to be ambushed by a random wobble in front of thirty people. And two sources quietly disagreeing is a properly journalistic point to be able to make. It has its own slide.

Frankfurter would have failed in the browser. The endpoint in the plan redirects to a new domain, and the redirect response carries no Access-Control-Allow-Origin header, so a browser blocks it before it ever reaches the working endpoint. The kind of thing that looks fine when you paste it into a browser bar and fails the moment an app tries it. The matrix now carries the destination URL.

Two smaller gaps closed on the way. The safety briefing promised three points and listed two, so the missing third, that a public link is public, is restored as rule 03 and now bookends properly with the sharing slide at the end. And the plan never answered the question someone always asks: why say never paste a key, then ask everyone to call Gemini from inside their app? That gets its own slide immediately after the rules, rather than a raised hand halfway through Part 3.

The one thing added without being asked is a participant pre-flight at slide 2. Three checks, two minutes, at the very top of the session. The plan's pre-flight was facilitator-only, and the likeliest way to lose a workshop is finding out at the halfway mark that four people can't open Canvas at all.

Checked at 1920×1080 across all thirty-two slides: no overflow, no console errors. The appendix stacks and the second matrix slide needed a tighter setting to fit, which sits in the stylesheet as a measured correction rather than a guess.
